01 The one-line pitch

GivsiaTech (by Givsia Private Limited) is a software studio that sells four things — websites, AI automation, SaaS builds, and mobile app development — through a single platform that runs the entire client relationship end to end: request → pay → track → review → and, once delivered, the finished project becomes public portfolio proof that sells the next client.

The interesting part for an interview isn't "it's a website with a dashboard" — it's that the same platform plays three different roles depending on who's logged in: a storefront for visitors, an operations system for the company (orders, invoicing, renewals, moderation), and a growth engine for outbound sales (AI-assisted cold outreach, grounded in the company's real, current data so it never overpromises).

02 Business model — how it makes money

Two revenue shapes, both modeled directly in the Order schema:

Revenue typeHow it's capturedWhy it matters
Project-based (primary) Client pays via Razorpay checkout for a fixed-scope build (service: website / AI automation / SaaS / app development / other). Payment is verified server-side by re-computing the Razorpay HMAC signature before an order is ever marked paid — never trusts the frontend's word for it. This is the core, one-time services revenue — the studio's bread and butter.
Recurring / renewal (secondary) Every delivered project can carry domain, hostingProvider, domainExpiryDate, hostingExpiryDate. These surface in a dedicated admin Renewals tab so the team can proactively reach out before a client's domain/hosting lapses. Turns a one-time build into an ongoing relationship — maintenance/hosting renewal revenue instead of a single transaction.

Accuracy note for interviews: renewal tracking today is a data field surfaced to the admin for manual follow-up — there's no automated reminder email/cron job yet. That's a natural "here's what I'd build next" answer if asked about roadmap.

Pricing is content, not code

Pricing tiers (Pricing model: name, price, description, feature list, "featured" flag, display order) are admin-editable data, not hardcoded numbers in the frontend. That means the business can change pricing, run promotions, or add a tier without a deploy — a real "run the business without an engineer" capability worth mentioning.

Two sales channels feed the same pipe

  • Inbound / online: a visitor registers, requests a project, and pays through the site themselves (source: "online").
  • Outbound / offline: a deal closed by phone, in person, or via cold outreach is logged manually by an admin (source: "offline", with an optional manualClient — name, phone, email — for a walk-in customer who has no account at all).

Both paths land in the exact same Order collection and the exact same admin Orders tab — the business doesn't run two separate systems for "deals we closed ourselves" vs. "deals that came through the website."

06 AI as a business lever, not a gimmick

Two distinct AI touchpoints, and the design choice behind both is the same: ground every answer in the business's real, current data — never let the model invent a fact, a price, or a client name.

Givi — the on-site conversion assistant

A chat widget answers visitor questions about services, pricing, and past work by pulling live data from the same database the admin edits — so pricing/portfolio updates are instantly reflected in what the AI tells prospects, with zero risk of the bot quoting stale numbers. It's also wired to explain the site's own navigation and steer visitors toward "Start a project" or the contact form, functioning as a low-cost, always-on first line of sales qualification. A hard-coded privacy rule permanently blocks it from ever discussing accounts, credentials, payment records, or internal system details, even under a direct or "ignore your instructions" prompt.

AI-assisted cold outreach — the growth engine

The service role's entire job is outbound: generate a draft cold email via Gemini, grounded in the exact same live pricing/portfolio/company data, review and edit it, then send. Every send — successful or failed — is logged with the sender, recipient, and outcome, giving the business a full audit trail of outbound activity instead of outreach happening invisibly through someone's personal inbox.

Interview framing: "We didn't just bolt on a chatbot — we built one data-grounding pattern (buildKnowledgeContext) and reused it for both the customer-facing assistant and the internal sales-drafting tool, so the AI never says something the business itself doesn't actually offer."

07 Trust, governance & risk controls

Details worth having ready if asked "how do you know this is production-grade, not a toy project":

  • Server-side authorization, always. Every protected route independently re-checks the JWT and role — the frontend's role-based UI is convenience, not the security boundary.
  • Full activity audit log. A middleware layer records who did what, when, from where (method, path, action, status code, IP, timestamp), visible to admins in an Activity tab and expiring automatically after 30 days — the kind of accountability trail an enterprise buyer or auditor would expect.
  • Payment integrity. Payments are verified via HMAC signature recomputation, plus a Razorpay server-to-server webhook as a second, independent confirmation path — not reliant solely on the client-side checkout callback.
  • Founder-locked content. The public "Founders" section can only be edited by the specific admin account it's linked to — a third admin literally has no document to edit, which is what actually enforces "only the real founders can change their own bios," not just a UI convention.
  • Bounded self-service. Clients can cancel their own project, but only within a 2-day window enforced at the API level (not just hidden in the UI afterward) — balances customer flexibility against the business's need to not eat a cancellation on work already underway.
  • Review integrity. One review per client, enforced at the schema level (a unique index on the client field) — resubmitting edits your existing review rather than stacking up duplicates that could skew the average rating.
  • Automated threat response, not just logging. Failed logins, rate-limit trips, flagged spam/prompt-injection attempts, and rejected uploads all feed one detection log; an IP that trips 3 of them within an hour is auto-blocked for 24 hours, and a repeat offender after that is blocked permanently — with a live admin Security tab and email alerts on anything critical. Verified working end to end during development, including a real self-inflicted block during testing (see the Security Hardening entry in the Development Log).
  • AI-assisted content screening, honestly scoped. Contact-form and chatbot messages are screened by a fast heuristic layer plus, for ambiguous cases, the same Gemini model already used elsewhere in the app — deliberately described as zero-shot classification, not a trained fraud-detection model, anywhere it's discussed. A "suspicious" verdict is flagged for human review rather than auto-rejected, so a heuristic false positive can't silently turn away a real lead.
  • Money moves fail-safe, never fail-silent. A refund is only ever recorded once Razorpay's refund API call actually succeeds — a failed call leaves the order's state completely untouched and surfaces a clear error, instead of quietly promising a refund that didn't happen.
  • Discount limits enforced at the boundary, not the form. A client can undercut a service's listed price by at most ₹5,000 when requesting a project — re-derived and enforced in the payment-creation endpoint itself, not just disabled in the UI, so the real floor can't be bypassed by editing a request.
  • New data-sourcing features are scoped to what's actually lawful to automate. The AI Business Lead Finder discovers prospects via Google's official, paid Places API rather than scraping Google Maps or listing sites (both explicitly prohibit that in their terms); the New Business Monitor (newly registered companies) is import-only, since the relevant government registry has no free bulk API. Both limits are stated directly in the admin tool's own copy, not glossed over.

08 Why this stack — technical choice → business reason

Technical choiceBusiness reason
Role-based JWT auth (admin/client/service), invite-code-gated for admin/service One login system serves three completely different audiences without three separate apps to maintain.
Content (pricing, portfolio, about, founders) stored as editable data, not hardcoded Non-engineers on the team can update prices, add case studies, or change company copy without a developer or a deploy.
AI responses grounded in live DB data instead of a static prompt Removes the single biggest AI-adoption risk for a services business — the bot quoting a stale price or a client name it shouldn't know.
Dual logging (raw HTTP + semantic activity log) Operational debugging and business accountability are two different needs — the raw log helps engineers, the activity log helps the business answer "who changed this and when."
Manual/offline order entry alongside online checkout The platform doesn't force every sale through a self-serve checkout — it also captures deals closed the old-fashioned way, so the business has one single source of truth for all revenue, not two.
Invoice PDFs generated server-side (PDFKit), not a browser print dialog A client gets a real, consistent, downloadable document for their records — not a screenshot-quality browser print-out that looks different on every device.
Business lead discovery via Google's official Places API, not scraping Keeps an outbound growth feature on the right side of Google's terms of service from day one, instead of building on a data source that could be cut off or create legal exposure later.

09 Interview Q&A cheat sheet

"Walk me through what this project actually does."
It's a full-stack platform for a software services company: a public marketing site that converts visitors into paying clients, a client dashboard that runs the entire project lifecycle (request, pay, track, review), an admin backend that runs the business (orders, pricing, content, users, renewals), and a dedicated growth-ops role for AI-assisted outbound sales — all sharing one auth system and one database.
"What was the hardest business-logic decision, not just technical decision?"
Deciding what should gate a project going live in the public portfolio. Landing on "must be completed, must have an image, must have domain + hosting recorded" — encoded as actual validation in the publish endpoint, not just a checklist someone has to remember — means the public case-study section can never accidentally show unfinished or undocumented work.
"How does the AI actually make money for the business, concretely?"
Two ways: it lowers the cost of qualifying inbound visitors (Givi answers pricing/services questions instantly, 24/7, instead of a human doing first-touch triage), and it speeds up outbound prospecting (the service role drafts a grounded, on-brand cold email in seconds instead of a person writing one from scratch) — while every send is logged, so it's measurable, not a black box.
"What would you build next if you kept going?"
Automated renewal reminders — the data (domain/hosting expiry dates) is already tracked and surfaced to admins, but there's no scheduled job yet that emails a client proactively before their domain lapses. That's the clearest gap between "data we capture" and "revenue we actually convert on."
"How do you prevent the AI from misrepresenting the company?"
Every AI-facing feature shares one pattern: build the prompt context by querying the live Pricing/Portfolio/CompanyInfo collections at request time, and explicitly instruct the model never to invent numbers, names, or guarantees beyond what's given. It's the same function reused for both the customer chatbot and the internal outreach drafting tool, so there's one place that guarantee lives, not two.
"Why three roles instead of just admin and client?"
Because outbound sales/growth work is operationally different from running the business — a growth-ops person needs to send outreach emails but has no business reason to see client accounts, orders, or payment data. Splitting it into its own service role means the principle of least privilege is enforced by the role system itself, not by trusting someone not to click around.
"How do you find new customers, versus just waiting for them to find you?"
The AI Business Lead Finder — an admin-only tool that discovers real local businesses with no website via Google's official Places API, drops them into a full lead-management CRM (status pipeline, notes, activity timeline, follow-up scheduling), and drafts a grounded AI outreach email or proposal for each one. It turns admin from someone who only processes inbound requests into someone who can actively go find work.
"Isn't scraping business listings for leads legally risky?"
That's exactly why it doesn't scrape. Google Maps and listing sites like Justdial explicitly prohibit automated scraping in their terms regardless of intent, so discovery goes through Google's own paid Places API instead — the same data (name, address, phone, rating, review count, website presence), obtained the way Google actually allows. The same reasoning applies to the newly-registered-companies module: no free bulk API exists for that government registry, so that one is import-only rather than built on a scraper.

10 Outbound growth engine — AI Business Lead Finder

Section 06 covers AI as a conversion/efficiency lever for demand that already exists. This is the newer, complementary half: AI-assisted sourcing of demand that doesn't know GivsiaTech exists yet.

The gap it closes

Every mechanism described elsewhere in this document — Givi, the contact form, the outreach-drafting tool — only activates once someone has already found the site. For a services business selling to small/local businesses (the exact list this tool targets: restaurants, clinics, salons, gyms, real estate agents, and two dozen more categories), a large share of the addressable market never searches for a software studio at all — they simply don't have a website and don't know what having one would change. Someone has to go find them first.

How it's structured as a real sales tool, not a toy

  • Discovery, not a list dump. Businesses are found per category + city/country via Google Places, and anything that already has a real website is filtered out automatically before it ever reaches the pipeline — the tool only surfaces businesses that actually match the pitch.
  • One CRM engine, two modules. The same lead-status pipeline (New → Contacted → Follow-Up → Meeting → Proposal → Negotiation → Won/Lost/Do-Not-Contact), notes, and activity timeline serve both the website-discovery module and the newly-registered-business module — a sales rep learns one workflow, not two.
  • AI drafts, human sends. Every generated email or proposal is grounded in the same live pricing/portfolio data used sitewide, shown for review/editing, and never auto-sent — same trust pattern as the existing Service Dashboard outreach tool, just reused rather than reinvented.
  • Measurable, not a black box. Analytics (total/new-today/contacted/pending/follow-ups-due/ won/lost/conversion-rate, leads by category) turn "we're doing outbound now" into a number someone can report on weekly.

Scoped deliberately for a first pass: city/country are filters on one table rather than dozens of hardcoded per-city dashboards, and the newly-registered-business module is CSV-import-only pending a licensed registry data provider. Both are described in more implementation detail in DEVELOPMENT_LOG.html §18.
